\section{Privacy Analysis}
\label{sec:mia}

The cryptographic and differential-privacy guarantees of \cref{sec:privacy} protect the contributions and
the basin summaries, not the final model, which every party obtains in the clear after decryption. This is
not a gap a stronger mechanism could close. Any protocol that lets separate parties build a common model
must move knowledge between them, and a model that is useful to a client---one that classifies categories
the client never saw locally---necessarily encodes information about the data that taught it those
categories. No method that returns a shared, useful model can therefore promise that nothing about any
party's data is ever inferable from it. The meaningful question is not whether residual leakage is zero,
which it cannot be, but how large an attack surface a protocol exposes on the way to the model, and the
design of \cref{sec:method} is built to make that surface as small as a one-shot federated method can.

\subsection{What each protocol leaves exposed}
\label{sec:mia-surface}

Read as attack surfaces, the alternatives differ by orders of magnitude. Multi-round federated learning
exposes the most: every round publishes a fresh update computed on private data, so an honest-but-curious
server or peer accumulates a long sequence of views of each client over the course of training, and the
membership-inference literature targets exactly this repetition~\cite{shokri2017membership,nasr2019comprehensive}.
One-shot methods that lean on differential privacy expose, in a single object, essentially the whole of a
client's local knowledge: a differentially private one-shot pipeline that synthesizes data ships a full
synthetic copy of each client's dataset in the clear, and one that perturbs a model ships the noised model
itself. HE-IFD exposes the least it can while still producing a shared model. The per-client displacements
are encrypted under the joint key and never appear in the clear; the only object any party receives is the
single decrypted aggregate; and the only signal released in the open is the small set of per-class
prototypes, themselves under differential privacy. The bulk of every contribution is delegated to
encryption, and what remains in the open is one aggregate and a handful of private summaries.

\subsection{Measured residual on the open channels}
\label{sec:mia-measured}

We measure what remains with membership-inference attacks~\cite{yeom2018privacy,salem2019ml,carlini2022membership}
across the surfaces an adversary could realistically observe in our protocol: the released model, queried
externally or by a fellow client who brings its own data and the shared prototypes as a prior, and the
prototype channel itself. We include the attacks tailored to the federated-distillation
setting~\cite{yang2023fdleaks,wang2024graddiff,gu2023ldia,jagielski2023students}, and score by area under
the ROC curve, with the true-positive rate at a low fixed false-positive rate alongside.

On the released model the leakage stays small. On the language task the strongest shadow-model attack sits
at the level of random guessing across heterogeneity levels, so a participant learns essentially nothing
about another's membership from the shared model beyond what any usable classifier of that data would
reveal. The prototype channel is the one place a \emph{raw} release leaks---unsurprisingly, since a raw
prototype is an average of real features---and it is exactly the place differential privacy applies.
\Cref{tab:mia-proto} shows that the membership signal on the prototypes collapses to the level of guessing
once the budget is applied: at $\varepsilon=8$ and below the area under the curve returns to about one half
on both the vision and the language backbone. The in-the-clear channel is closed by the same
averaging-variant accounting that leaves accuracy flat (\cref{sec:privacy-eval}).

\subsection{The inherent limit}
\label{sec:mia-limit}

We do not claim the released model leaks nothing. A model that hands every client coverage of classes it
never observed must carry information about the data that supplied that coverage, and an adversary with
strong priors about a client's distribution can recover some of it; this is a property of useful shared
models, not of our protocol in particular. What the protocol guarantees is that this residual is the
\emph{entire} surface. Because the contributions are never exposed, the leakage a client faces is bounded by
what one shared model inherently reveals---not multiplied across the rounds of an iterative protocol, and
not handed over wholesale as a released synthetic dataset or a perturbed model. Driving the exposed surface
down to that inherent floor, rather than promising to remove a leak that cannot be removed when parties set
out to build a common model from their data, is the privacy goal the design is built to meet, and the
measurements above show it reaches that floor on the one channel that is open.
