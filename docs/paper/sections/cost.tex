\section{Efficiency}
\label{sec:fhe-cost}

The server's only cryptographic operation is the depth-one weighted sum of \cref{eq:aggregate}. We implement
it end to end in a real multiparty CKKS protocol on the Lattigo library~\cite{lattigo,mouchet2021multiparty}
--- distributed key generation, encryption under the joint key, the homomorphic aggregation, and threshold
decryption by a collective key-switch --- and measure what it costs. The cost is set entirely by the number
of \emph{fine-tuned} parameters that enter a ciphertext, not by the frozen backbone behind them, so a small
adapter and head are cheap whatever model they sit on.

\paragraph{Parameters and setup.} All figures use ring degree $2^{14}$ ($8192$ slots per ciphertext), a
modulus chain $\log Q=\{55,45\}$ with key-switch prime $\log P=\{61\}$, and scale $2^{45}$. The circuit is
multiplicative \emph{depth one} --- one plaintext-scalar multiplication and a rescale, with no relinearization
and no bootstrapping --- and decryption is $\Nc$-out-of-$\Nc$ (no single party can decrypt). Communication
figures are exact; timings are single-run wall-clock on one core of a commodity laptop CPU (Apple M4) and are
reported as indicative.

\paragraph{Communication.} A round is one upload and one download. The per-client traffic is fixed by the
trainable-parameter count: a unit of up to $8192$ parameters is a single $0.5$\,MiB ciphertext, and the
$100$-class vision head ($76.9$k parameters) spans ten. Total traffic scales linearly in the number of
clients (\cref{tab:cost-comm}). Even the largest head at $\Nc=100$ moves about half a gigabyte in total,
against the hundreds of gigabytes an encrypted full-model protocol would move.

\paragraph{Computation.} The server's homomorphic work is milliseconds at deployment scale and stays
sub-second even at $\Nc=100$ on the largest head (\cref{tab:cost-time}). The measured operations follow simple
laws: a client encrypts in about $2.2$\,ms per ciphertext (in parallel across clients), while the server
aggregation and the threshold decryption scale as $\approx\!0.39$ and $\approx\!0.23$\,ms per
$\Nc\times(\text{ciphertexts/client})$, and the one-time distributed key generation grows linearly in $\Nc$
at about $1$\,ms per party. There is no bootstrapping anywhere in the protocol.

\paragraph{The adapter budget.} Fine-tuning a low-rank adapter rather than a linear head changes the picture
only by the adapter's parameter count. A rank-$r$ adapter on a $d$-dimensional layer adds $2dr$ parameters, so
adapting a handful of layers adds on the order of $10^{4}$ parameters --- a small, fixed number of additional
ciphertexts on top of the head. The encrypted object stays a few megabytes per client, the circuit stays
depth one, and the server cost grows only with that ciphertext count, never with the size of the frozen
backbone the adapter sits in.

\paragraph{Correctness.} The decrypted aggregate matches the plaintext computation to a relative $\ell_2$
error between $1.4\times10^{-9}$ ($\Nc=5$) and $2.6\times10^{-8}$ ($\Nc=100$). This is the encoding precision
of CKKS at scale $2^{45}$; it grows mildly with $\Nc$ as ciphertext additions and the key-switch smudging
noise accumulate, and it stays several orders of magnitude below any tolerance the decoded model is sensitive
to. Because the circuit is depth one, the error is not amplified.

\paragraph{Comparison with prior cryptographic federated learning.} The contrast with prior homomorphic
federated learning is structural, not incremental. Encrypted-training methods such as
POSEIDON~\cite{sav2021poseidon} reach comparable accuracy but through many rounds of homomorphic computation
that the source paper reports at roughly $1.4$ hours of wall-clock; secure-aggregation
primitives~\cite{kemmaka2025hybagg} sum updates once per round inside an iterative protocol. Our protocol runs
in a single round whose server computation is milliseconds and whose communication is a few megabytes,
because the only thing encrypted is a bounded fine-tuning displacement of a small adapter, combined at
multiplicative depth one. The broader method-level comparison is in \cref{tab:positioning}.
