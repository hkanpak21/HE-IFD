\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Setting and Overview}
\label{sec:setting}

We consider $\Nc$ clients that wish to train a shared classifier together but cannot pool their data.
Client $j$ holds a private labelled dataset $\Dj$ of size $\nj$ over a common label space of $\Cc$
classes. The data are heterogeneous: the class proportions on each client follow a Dirichlet
distribution with concentration $\alpha$, and a smaller $\alpha$ means each client observes fewer classes
and the partition is more skewed. Two constraints separate our setting from ordinary federated learning.
The protocol is \emph{one-shot}: each client uploads once and downloads once, with no iterative exchange
of model updates. And privacy is \emph{cryptographic}: a client's contribution is never revealed in the
clear to the server or to any other party, and we are unwilling to buy that privacy with the accuracy loss
that differential privacy incurs when it is the only line of defence.

Every client uses a model of the same form, namely a frozen feature extractor $\featx$ followed by a small
trainable head $\head$ with parameters $\headp$, composed as $\model = \head \circ \featx$. The feature
extractor is either a publicly available pretrained backbone or a from-scratch architecture fixed in
advance; it never changes, is never transmitted, and is never encrypted. Only the head is trained,
communicated, or operated on under encryption. Keeping the trainable part this small is what makes the
encrypted step inexpensive: a classifier head is a few thousand scalars, so a client's entire encrypted
contribution fits in a single ciphertext, as we show in \cref{sec:aggregation}. It is natural to ask
whether so small a trainable part limits accuracy; \cref{sec:experiments} shows that it does not, and that
adding trainable capacity does not improve the result in this regime.

HE-IFD proceeds in three stages, illustrated in \cref{fig:protocol}. First, before any local training, the clients agree on a common
initialization $\thz$ for the head using only lightweight per-class summaries exchanged over secure
peer-to-peer channels, with the server taking no part. Second, starting from $\thz$, each client distils
its own local teacher into the shared head for a bounded number of steps and records the displacement it
has travelled, $\disp = \theta_j^{(\Ksteps)} - \thz$. Third, the server combines the encrypted
displacements into a single global model, $\thstar = \thz + \sum_j \wj \disp$, using only homomorphic
additions and multiplications by public scalars, after which a threshold of clients jointly decrypts the
result. The three stages are the subjects of \cref{sec:basin,sec:distill,sec:aggregation}.

\caption{One-shot federated distillation with encrypted aggregation. $\mathsf{Enc}(\cdot)$ denotes
encryption under the clients' collective CKKS key.}
\label{alg:protocol}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require clients $1,\dots,\Nc$ with data $\Dj$; frozen extractor $\featx$; steps $\Ksteps$; temperature $\temp$
\Statex \textit{Phase 0 --- shared loss basin (peer-to-peer; server excluded)}
\State each client computes per-class feature means through $\featx$, optionally under $(\varepsilon,\delta)$-DP
\State clients exchange and pool the summaries, and warm-start the head to obtain $\thz$
\State clients run distributed key generation, yielding a collective CKKS key
\Statex \textit{Local distillation --- each client $j$ in parallel}
\State train the local teacher $\teacher$ on $\Dj$;\quad $\theta_j^{(0)} \gets \thz$
\For{$k = 1$ to $\Ksteps$}
  \State $\theta_j^{(k)} \gets \theta_j^{(k-1)} - \eta\,\nabla\,\KL\!\big(\sigma_{\temp}(\teacher)\,\|\,\sigma_{\temp}(\model)\big)$
\EndFor
\State $\disp \gets \theta_j^{(\Ksteps)} - \thz$;\quad upload $\mathsf{Enc}(\disp)$
\Statex \textit{Encrypted aggregation --- server}
\State $\mathsf{Enc}(\thstar) \gets \thz + \sum_j \wj\cdot\mathsf{Enc}(\disp)$ \Comment{PT$\times$CT, CT+CT; depth $1$}
\State broadcast $\mathsf{Enc}(\thstar)$
\Statex \textit{Threshold decryption --- clients}
\State a threshold of clients jointly decrypt $\mathsf{Enc}(\thstar)$ to recover $\thstar$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

What we are after is not a model that outperforms each client on that client's own dominant classes; a
local specialist trained on a narrow slice of the label space is hard to beat on that slice, and we do not
claim to. We are after a common model that every party can use, that classifies well across the whole
label space including classes a given client never observed locally, and that is produced without any
party exposing its data or its update in the clear.

\subsection{The Shared Loss Basin}
\label{sec:basin}

The natural one-shot recipe is to let each client train its head independently and have the server average
the results. Under heterogeneity this fails. When each client begins from its own initialization and fits
its own skewed local distribution, the trained heads come to occupy different regions of parameter space,
and their average lies in none of them. The per-client updates point in conflicting directions, so summing
them cancels signal rather than reinforcing it, and the combined model is no better, and often worse, than
its parts. We quantify this conflict directly in \cref{sec:experiments}.

We remove the conflict at its source by having every client begin from the same head initialization
$\thz$, which we call the shared loss basin. The term is chosen deliberately. We do not require $\thz$ to
be an accurate classifier; its role is to place all clients in a common region of the loss landscape so
that the directions they subsequently move in, their displacements $\disp$, live in one shared frame and
add constructively rather than fighting one another. In this setting a weak but shared starting point is
worth more than a strong but private one. The distinction is central to the paper, and
\cref{sec:experiments} shows its two consequences in turn: without a shared basin the one-shot combination
collapses, and even a deliberately weak basin is enough for the distillation that follows to yield a strong
common model.

Constructing $\thz$ must not leak the data we set out to protect, so clients never exchange examples.
Instead, over secure peer-to-peer channels from which the server is excluded, each client computes the
average feature vector of each class it holds, taken through the frozen extractor $\featx$, and shares only
these per-class summaries. The summaries are pooled into a small aligned set on which the shared head is
warm-started to obtain $\thz$. Because the only quantity that leaves a client is a per-class average, the
leakage is small and is simple to bound: when stronger guarantees are required, the averages are released
under an $(\varepsilon,\delta)$ differential-privacy mechanism whose noise is calibrated to the averaging
sensitivity, which we detail in \cref{sec:privacy}.

What matters for the protocol is that a shared, geometry-aligning $\thz$ is produced cheaply, not how it is
produced. We therefore treat the basin source as a deployment choice and evaluate several interchangeable
instantiations in \cref{sec:experiments}: a small public probe set; differentially-private per-class
prototypes computed from a small fraction of local data; per-client synthetic samples; and, at the extreme,
a basin formed with no labelled public data at all. Each serves the same purpose, and the protocol that
follows is identical in every case.

\subsection{Local Bounded-Trajectory Distillation}
\label{sec:distill}

Each client first trains a local teacher $\teacher$ on its own data $\Dj$. The teacher is a strong
specialist on the classes the client actually holds, but it is also private and idiosyncratic: it encodes
that client's particular class mixture and cannot be shared or averaged with other teachers directly. The
purpose of the next stage is to transfer what the teacher knows into the shared head, in a form that can be
combined across clients.

Starting from the shared basin $\thz$, client $j$ runs $\Ksteps$ steps of knowledge distillation, training
the head to match its teacher's softened predictions by minimising
$\KL\!\left(\sigma_{\temp}(\teacher) \,\|\, \sigma_{\temp}(\model)\right)$, where $\sigma_{\temp}$ is the
softmax at temperature $\temp$. The number of steps $\Ksteps$ is deliberately bounded and small. This is
not an optimisation shortcut but a requirement of the method: a short trajectory keeps the head close to
$\thz$, and therefore inside the shared basin, so that the displacement it produces is still expressed in
the common frame established in \cref{sec:basin}. Allowed to train to convergence, each client would drift
to its own local optimum and reintroduce exactly the conflict that the shared basin was constructed to
remove. The temperature $\temp$ and the step budget $\Ksteps$ are the two hyperparameters of this stage;
we use modest fixed values throughout and show in \cref{sec:experiments} that the gain is robust to their
choice.

At the end of its trajectory the client has parameters $\theta_j^{(\Ksteps)}$. Rather than the parameters
themselves, the client retains and will transmit the displacement
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:displacement}
\disp \;=\; \theta_j^{(\Ksteps)} - \thz ,
\end{equation}
the cumulative distance it has travelled from the shared basin. Because every client starts from the same
$\thz$, these displacements are directly comparable across clients, which is what makes the linear
combination in the next stage meaningful.

\subsection{Encrypted Linear Aggregation}
\label{sec:aggregation}

The clients hold a joint homomorphic-encryption key produced by a distributed key-generation protocol, so
that no single party, and in particular not the server, ever holds the secret key. Each client encrypts its
displacement $\disp$, the bounded trajectory of improvement it computed in \cref{sec:distill}; since the
head is only a few thousand scalars, this fits in a single ciphertext. The server's task is to fuse these
per-client trajectories into a single update of the shared model, which it does by the sample-weighted
combination
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:aggregate}
\thstar \;=\; \thz + \sum_{j=1}^{\Nc} \wj \, \disp ,
\qquad \wj = \frac{\nj}{\sum_{i} n_i},
\end{equation}
in which each client's trajectory contributes in proportion to the data $\nj$ behind it, with the weights
$\wj$ public scalars. Because every $\disp$ is expressed in the common frame of the shared basin
(\cref{sec:basin}), \cref{eq:aggregate} is a consensus over the clients' directions of improvement rather
than an average of unrelated weight vectors: the trajectories reinforce on the components where clients
agree and cancel where they conflict, and the bounded, basin-anchored construction of \cref{sec:distill} is
what keeps that consensus inside a region where the model is good.

This fusion is realised entirely with the two cheapest homomorphic operations. Forming \cref{eq:aggregate}
requires only multiplying each ciphertext by the public scalar $\wj$ and adding the results, with the
public basin $\thz$ added as a plaintext; there is no multiplication of one ciphertext by another and no
bootstrapping, so the circuit has multiplicative depth one. A threshold of clients then jointly decrypts
$\thstar$ by collective key-switching, and the server never observes a plaintext update at any point. This
is the design choice on which the whole approach turns: by casting aggregation as a fusion of update
trajectories that is \emph{linear in the encrypted quantities}, we obtain a server computation that maps
onto the cheapest operations a levelled scheme provides, free of the encrypted nonlinearities and
polynomial activations that make encrypted training expensive. We instantiate \cref{eq:aggregate} in a real
multiparty CKKS implementation and report its measured correctness and cost in \cref{sec:experiments}.

It is worth being precise about why this works, because \cref{eq:aggregate} can be read too quickly. Since
the weights sum to one, the displacement form is algebraically equal to a weighted average of the clients'
final heads, $\thstar = \sum_j \wj\, \theta_j^{(\Ksteps)}$. This linearity is what keeps the operation
inexpensive to encrypt. But the same average computed over heads that were trained independently, from
different initializations, to convergence, is precisely the naive combination that fails in
\cref{sec:basin}. What makes the average land in a good region here is not the averaging but the protocol
around it: every $\theta_j^{(\Ksteps)}$ starts from the shared basin $\thz$ and moves only a bounded number
of steps, so all of them remain in the common frame and their weighted average is itself a sensible model.
The contribution is therefore not a new aggregation rule but a one-shot protocol under which a linear, and
hence cheaply encryptable, aggregation actually produces a strong shared model.

\subsection{Privacy}
\label{sec:privacy}

The protocol protects privacy in two layers, applied to two different objects. The model updates
themselves are protected cryptographically. The aggregation of \cref{eq:aggregate} runs entirely under
multiparty CKKS with threshold decryption, so the displacements $\disp$ are never exposed to the server or
to any minority of clients, and they are protected without any perturbation: the cryptographic layer is
adds no accuracy cost. This is the main privacy claim of the paper and the point on which it
departs from differentially private one-shot federated learning, which injects noise into the model updates
that are ultimately released and therefore pays a utility tax on the very thing it ships.

The second layer concerns the only data-derived quantities that leave a client before encryption, namely
the per-class feature averages used to build the shared basin in \cref{sec:basin}. These are released under
the averaging variant of the Gaussian mechanism. Averaging $\Kpc$ samples per class before release lowers
the sensitivity of the summary in proportion to $1/\Kpc$, so the noise needed for an $(\varepsilon,\delta)$
guarantee scales as $\mathrm{clip}/(\Kpc\,\varepsilon)$; a client buys privacy budget by averaging more
samples rather than by degrading the model it contributes. Confining the perturbed quantity to these small
summaries, and protecting the actual updates with cryptography that adds no noise, is what lets the method offer
a formal $(\varepsilon,\delta)$ guarantee at little cost in accuracy. In \cref{sec:experiments} we trace
this frontier and find that accuracy is essentially unchanged across the practical range of privacy
budgets, from the non-private setting through the single-digit $\varepsilon$ typical of differentially
private learning, with a decline only under the most stringent budget we consider ($\varepsilon=0.5$);
$\Kpc \geq 5$ suffices there.

Two parties are assumed throughout: an honest-but-curious server that follows the protocol but may try to
infer information from what it sees, and clients a minority of which may collude. The cryptographic layer
defends against both, since the server only ever handles ciphertexts and no minority below the decryption
threshold can recover a plaintext update. What the protocol does not by itself characterise is how much can
be inferred from the \emph{released} global model, which every party obtains. We measure that residual
leakage separately through membership-inference attacks in \cref{sec:mia}.

\subsection{Security Analysis}
\label{sec:security}

We make the privacy guarantee precise. Let the view of the honest-but-curious server during one execution
be everything it receives, namely the public collective key, the public weights $\{\wj\}$ and basin $\thz$,
the client ciphertexts $\{\mathsf{Enc}(\disp)\}_{j=1}^{\Nc}$, and the homomorphically computed
$\mathsf{Enc}(\thstar)$. We write $\mathsf{view}_{\mathrm{srv}}(\{\Dj\})$ for this view as a function of the
clients' private datasets.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:server}
Under the IND-CPA security of the multiparty CKKS scheme and its $t$-out-of-$\Nc$ threshold decryption, for
any honest-but-curious server colluding with up to $t-1$ clients there is a probabilistic polynomial-time
simulator $\mathcal{S}$, given only the public parameters, such that
$\mathsf{view}_{\mathrm{srv}}(\{\Dj\}) \stackrel{c}{\approx} \mathcal{S}$. In particular the server's view is
computationally independent of the private datasets $\{\Dj\}$ and of the displacements $\{\disp\}$, and the
server cannot recover any plaintext, including $\thstar$, without the cooperation of a decryption quorum.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
The simulator outputs the public parameters together with $\Nc$ encryptions of zero under the collective
key. By IND-CPA each genuine ciphertext $\mathsf{Enc}(\disp)$ is computationally indistinguishable from
$\mathsf{Enc}(0)$; a standard hybrid argument over the $\Nc$ ciphertexts replaces them one at a time, so the
whole collection is indistinguishable from the simulated one and hence carries no information about
$\{\disp\}$ or the data they derive from. The aggregate $\mathsf{Enc}(\thstar)$ is a deterministic function
of those ciphertexts and the public values $\{\wj\},\thz$, so it adds nothing to the view. Finally,
decryption needs $t$ shares of the secret key, which is never reconstructed in one place; a coalition of the
server and at most $t-1$ clients holds too few shares to decrypt any ciphertext.
\end{proof}

\Cref{prop:server} pins down where information can and cannot flow. The server never sees plaintext data,
plaintext updates, or the secret key; everything it touches is encrypted under a key it cannot use alone.
Consequently the protocol exposes private information through exactly two channels, both of them intrinsic
to the task rather than artifacts of our construction. The first is the Phase-0 alignment. The per-class
summaries are the only data-derived quantities released before encryption, they travel over peer-to-peer
channels and never reach the server, and what they reveal is precisely what the chosen alignment mechanism
reveals: a per-class mean in the raw case, an $(\varepsilon,\delta)$-bounded mean under the differentially
private variant, synthetic samples under the synthetic variant, and nothing labelled at all in the no-probe
case. This is a deliberate knob, traded against utility in \cref{sec:basin-contribution}. The second channel
is the released model itself: after threshold decryption every client holds $\thstar$ in the clear.
White-box access to the jointly built model is unavoidable in any protocol whose purpose is to hand the
parties a shared model, and the residual information it carries about training data is what we quantify with
membership inference in \cref{sec:mia}. No third channel exists.

\subsection{Communication and Computation Cost}
\label{sec:cost}

The protocol is inexpensive and scales well with the number of clients. Each client sends one ciphertext and receives
one: because a classifier head of $d$ parameters fits in a single CKKS ciphertext whenever $d$ is at most the
slot count, a client's upload and download are each $O(d)$ and, crucially, independent of the frozen
backbone behind the head. The entire exchange is a single round. The server's work is $\Nc$ multiplications
of a ciphertext by a public scalar followed by $\Nc$ ciphertext additions, an $O(\Nc d)$ computation of
multiplicative depth one with no bootstrapping. Both the per-client cost and the round count are therefore
constant in the depth of the model, in contrast to encrypted-training approaches whose cost grows with the
number of rounds and with a deep per-step circuit, and to secure-aggregation primitives that repeat over an
iterative protocol.

The structure is also highly parallel. The clients' teacher training, distillation, and encryption are
mutually independent and run simultaneously, and the server's aggregation is a sum, which is associative and
admits a tree reduction, so end-to-end wall-clock is essentially one client's local computation plus a
single aggregation regardless of $\Nc$. We report measured times and communication in \cref{sec:fhe-cost}
and compare them against prior systems in \cref{sec:positioning}.
