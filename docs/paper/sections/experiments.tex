\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

Our evaluation answers, in order, what the method delivers, why each part of it is needed, what the shared
basin contributes, and what privacy and communication cost the cryptographic protocol carries. Throughout,
the question we are asking is not whether the global model attains the highest possible test accuracy, but
whether the clients can jointly produce a single model that is stronger and more broadly capable than what
any of them could obtain alone, and whether they can do so under one round of communication and accuracy-preserving
cryptographic privacy.

\subsection{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

We evaluate on two regimes that bracket realistic deployments. In the from-scratch regime the feature
extractor is a small network trained as part of the protocol: a multilayer perceptron on MNIST and a
LeNet-style convolutional network on Fashion-MNIST~\cite{xiao2017fashion}. In the pretrained regime the
feature extractor is a fixed, publicly available backbone whose features are read off without fine-tuning:
a ViT-B/32 vision transformer on CIFAR-100~\cite{krizhevsky2009learning}, and the RoBERTa-base and MPNet
sentence encoders on AG-News. Data are partitioned
across clients by a Dirichlet distribution over labels with concentration parameter $\alpha$ (the standard label-skew
partition of federated learning: a smaller $\alpha$ leaves each client a few dominant classes and almost
none of the rest); we report the heterogeneous ($\alpha=0.05$), mild ($\alpha=0.3$), and near-homogeneous
($\alpha=1.0$) settings. Unless
stated otherwise there are $\Nc=10$ clients, the shared basin is built from raw per-class prototypes, the
local distillation runs for a small fixed number of steps $\Ksteps$ at temperature $\temp$, and every
number is averaged over three seeds. \Cref{tab:setup} summarises the configuration.

We report the test accuracy of the global model, the accuracy of the average and best local teacher as a
reference for what a client obtains on its own, the accuracy of a centralized model trained on the pooled
data as an upper bound, the accuracy of the shared basin $\thz$ on its own, and the coverage of
locally-unseen classes, defined as a client's accuracy on the classes for which it held no local examples.

\subsection{From Local Specialists to a Shared Global Model}
\label{sec:superior}

The main experiment shows that the protocol turns a set of narrow local teachers into a single model that is
strong across the whole label space. \Cref{fig:scaling} shows this under heterogeneous data
($\alpha=0.05$) for the pretrained backbones. On each task the average local teacher is weak, because under
this partition a client sees only a fraction of the classes and its teacher specialises accordingly: the
mean teacher accuracy is $0.23$ on CIFAR-100 and below $0.46$ on AG-News. The global model produced by the
protocol reaches $0.81$ on CIFAR-100 and $0.85$ on AG-News, between three and four times the average local
teacher, and lands within a few points of the centralized reference that was allowed to pool all the data.
A client that participates therefore walks away with a model far better than the one it could train alone,
and close to what it would have obtained had all the data been centralised, while never having shared that
data.

This behaviour is not confined to one backbone scale. The same protocol, with the same bounded distillation
and the same linear encrypted aggregation, holds from a small from-scratch MLP up to a frozen vision
transformer and a sentence-transformer language encoder. Because the feature extractor is frozen and never
enters the encrypted combination, moving to a larger backbone changes only the quality of the features the
small head sees, not the protocol or its cost; the head, and hence the encrypted object, stays the same
size. This is what lets the approach scale to modern pretrained models without scaling the cryptographic
work, and it is the reason a single classifier head, rather than a fine-tuned backbone, is the right unit
to encrypt.



\subsection{Scalability and the Effect of Trajectory Length}
\label{sec:scaling-ablation}

Two properties the protocol relies on hold up under direct test. \Cref{fig:nscaling} varies the number of
clients from five to fifty on ViT-B/32 over CIFAR-100 and shows the global model's accuracy is essentially
flat, and if anything improves slightly, as clients are added; more participants do not destabilise the
single linear aggregation. \Cref{fig:ksweep} varies the length of the distillation trajectory on ResNet-18
over CIFAR-10. With the temperature that keeps the teacher's confident predictions visible, accuracy is
highest at a short trajectory and declines gently as the trajectory lengthens, confirming that the bounded
$\Ksteps$ is doing real work: a longer trajectory lets each client drift away from the shared basin and the
aggregate degrades toward it. The figure also shows that an over-smoothed teacher signal (a high
temperature) erases the gain entirely and worsens with every additional step, which is why we keep both the
step budget small and the temperature low.

\subsection{Regularizing the Aggregated Displacement}
\label{sec:lambda}

The trajectory length $\Ksteps$ is one of two levers that keep clients inside the shared basin; the other is
how far the server moves the model along the combined displacement. Writing the aggregate with an explicit
scaling coefficient,
\[
  \thstar(\lambda) \;=\; \thz + \lambda \sum_j \wj \disp ,
\]
exposes the task-vector scaling coefficient of model merging~\cite{ilharco2023editing}: $\lambda$ sets the
magnitude of the step away from the basin, and because it is a public scalar the encrypted aggregation stays
at multiplicative depth one whatever value it takes. We sweep $\lambda$ from $0$ to $2$ by re-scoring the
same trajectories, with no retraining. The behaviour splits cleanly by heterogeneity. On near-homogeneous
data ($\alpha=1.0$) the optimum is exactly $\lambda=1$: the per-client displacements already agree, and
applying the combined displacement in full is best. Under severe heterogeneity ($\alpha=0.05$) the optimum
moves below $1$ and accuracy declines once $\lambda$ passes $1$, the same shape the trajectory-length sweep
shows in \cref{fig:ksweep}. Both report one mechanism: when clients fit very different local distributions,
their displacements applied at full strength carry the aggregate too far out of the common basin, and the
combined step has to be regularized. Read this way $\lambda$ is a drift regularizer, the continuous
counterpart of the bounded step budget, and treating it as one rather than pinning it at $1$ recovers a few
points of accuracy in exactly the heterogeneous regime where drift is worst---on MNIST the heterogeneous
optimum adds $2.4$ points over $\lambda=1$---at no cryptographic cost, with the same downward shift of the
optimum holding on the pretrained backbone. The coefficient never settles above $1$, which is the
operative statement: under heterogeneity the displacement should be damped toward the basin, never
amplified away from it.



\subsection{Comparison with Prior Work}
\label{sec:positioning}

\Cref{tab:positioning} places the method among representative federated-learning approaches along the axes
that matter here: whether the protocol is genuinely one-shot, what kind of privacy it provides on the
contributions, how many communication rounds it needs, and its principal limitation relative to our goal.
Existing one-shot distillation methods are either plaintext, and so offer no privacy on the contributions,
or differentially private, and so pay a utility cost on the released model. The homomorphic-encryption
methods in federated learning either run for many rounds, as in encrypted training, or are
secure-aggregation primitives that sum model updates over an iterative protocol rather than distilling in a
single shot. To our knowledge the combination we occupy, a one-shot federated fine-tuning whose
aggregation is protected by homomorphic encryption that adds no accuracy cost, has not previously been demonstrated.

Two comparisons are especially informative. Against POSEIDON, the closest cryptographic peer, the accuracy
is comparable on MNIST, but POSEIDON reaches it through multi-round encrypted training that the source paper
reports at roughly $1.4$ hours of wall-clock, whereas our protocol completes in a single round whose server
computation is milliseconds and whose communication is a few megabytes. Against the differentially private
one-shot methods, the natural utility peers, the contrast is that their accuracy is bought at a privacy
budget that perturbs the released model, while our encryption perturbs nothing. The communication advantage
is large and structural: a full-model encrypted protocol of the kind the rejected literature assumed would
move on the order of hundreds of gigabytes, whereas our single classifier-head ciphertext is a few
megabytes per round (\cref{sec:fhe-cost}). The protocol is also fully parallel across clients and
its aggregation is an associative reduction, so wall-clock does not grow with the number
of participants.

\subsection{The Incentive to Participate}
\label{sec:incentive}

It is worth being explicit about what a client gains. The global model does not, in general, beat a
client's own teacher on the handful of classes that client holds in abundance; a specialist trained on a
narrow distribution is hard to beat on that distribution, and our aim is not to. What the client gains is
coverage of the classes it never saw locally. Under heterogeneous data the global model classifies the
locally-unseen classes well above chance, whereas the client's own teacher cannot classify them at all,
having never been trained on them. The value of participating is therefore the breadth that a single
party cannot obtain on its own, delivered as a model every party can use, and obtained without exposing any
party's data or update in the clear.

\subsection{Scope and Limitations}
\label{sec:scope}

Two boundaries of the approach are worth stating plainly, as both follow from well-understood properties
rather than from incidental shortcomings. First, the method aggregates displacements of a head on top of a
frozen extractor, so it inherits the quality of that extractor's features: it works best when the frozen
representation is already linearly informative for the task. Representations from models trained for
next-token prediction are, by their nature, a weaker fit for discriminative classification than those from
encoders trained bidirectionally, and we accordingly use bidirectional sentence encoders for the text tasks;
the choice of a strong frozen extractor is part of the method, not a tuning detail. Second, the difficulty
the shared basin addresses, conflicting client updates under heterogeneity, becomes more severe as the data
grow more skewed; this is an inherent property of combining independently trained models in one shot, and
the basin is our means of containing it rather than a claim that it disappears. The regime where the basin
helps most is precisely the heterogeneous regime where it is most needed, and the experiments above are run
in exactly that regime.
