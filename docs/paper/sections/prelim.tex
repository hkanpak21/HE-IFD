\subsection{Multiparty Homomorphic Encryption}
\label{sec:mhe}

We build on the CKKS scheme~\cite{cheon2017ckks}, a ring-learning-with-errors
construction~\cite{lyubashevsky2010ideal} that performs approximate arithmetic on vectors of real numbers
under encryption. CKKS packs many real slots into one ciphertext and supports two homomorphic operations of
interest here: addition of two ciphertexts, and multiplication of a ciphertext by a plaintext. It is a
levelled scheme, meaning each multiplication consumes one level of a finite budget; our protocol uses only
additions and multiplications by public plaintext scalars, so it consumes a single level and never requires
the expensive bootstrapping step~\cite{cheon2018bootstrapping} that refreshes a depleted budget.

A single-key scheme would require one party to hold the secret key and therefore to be trusted with
decryption. We instead use the multiparty variant of CKKS~\cite{mouchet2021multiparty}, in which the secret
key is shared among the clients through a distributed key-generation protocol that produces a collective
public key without ever materialising the secret in one place. Any party, including the server, may compute
on ciphertexts under the collective public key, but decryption requires the cooperation of a threshold of
clients, carried out as a collective key-switching protocol. No single party, and in particular not the
server, can decrypt on its own. This is precisely the property our aggregation needs: the server performs
the homomorphic computation on encrypted client contributions, yet only the clients, acting together, can
reveal the result.

\subsection{Differential Privacy of Averaged Summaries}
\label{sec:dp}

A randomised mechanism $M$ is $(\varepsilon,\delta)$-differentially private if for all adjacent inputs
differing in one record and all measurable $S$, $\Pr[M(x)\in S]\le e^{\varepsilon}\Pr[M(x')\in S]+\delta$.
The Gaussian mechanism achieves this for a vector-valued query by adding noise
$\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2 I)$ with $\sigma \propto \Delta_2\sqrt{2\ln(1.25/\delta)}/\varepsilon$, where
$\Delta_2$ is the $\ell_2$ sensitivity of the query, the largest change in its output from altering one
record.

The only data-derived quantities released before encryption in our protocol are per-class feature
averages, and we use this structure to our advantage. If each released summary is the mean of $\Kpc$
clipped per-example features, then changing one example changes the mean by at most $\mathrm{clip}/\Kpc$,
so the sensitivity is $\Delta_2 = \mathrm{clip}/\Kpc$ and the required noise scales as
$\sigma \propto \mathrm{clip}/(\Kpc\,\varepsilon)$. Averaging more samples per class lowers the sensitivity
and hence the noise, so a client tightens its privacy budget by averaging over more of its data rather than
by accepting a worse contribution. We refer to this as the averaging variant of the Gaussian mechanism and
apply it to the Phase-0 prototypes in \cref{sec:basin,sec:privacy}.

\subsection{Threat Model}
\label{sec:threat}

The participants are $\Nc$ clients and a single aggregation server. The server is honest-but-curious: it
follows the protocol faithfully but may attempt to infer private information from anything it observes.
During aggregation the server sees only ciphertexts under the collective key, and as established in
\cref{sec:mhe} it cannot decrypt them; it is moreover excluded entirely from the shared-basin construction,
which the clients carry out among themselves over secure, authenticated peer-to-peer channels. A minority
of clients may likewise be honest-but-curious and may collude, but any coalition below the decryption
threshold learns nothing about another client's displacement, since recovering a plaintext requires the
cooperation of the threshold. This is a stronger position than secure-aggregation protocols, where the
revealed sum has itself been shown to leak across rounds~\cite{so2023securing,kerkouche2023client}, because
in our one-shot protocol the server never obtains a plaintext sum at all.

Three properties follow. Raw data never leave their client. The model updates are protected by encryption
alone and are therefore released to the aggregation without any accuracy-reducing perturbation. And the
small per-class summaries used to form the shared basin, the only data-derived values exposed before
encryption, are released under the differential-privacy mechanism of \cref{sec:dp}. What this model does
not on its own quantify is how much an adversary can infer from the final global model, which every party
receives in the clear after decryption; we measure that residual exposure directly with membership-inference
attacks in \cref{sec:mia}. Robustness to actively malicious or Byzantine clients is outside our scope and we
return to it only as a direction for future work.
